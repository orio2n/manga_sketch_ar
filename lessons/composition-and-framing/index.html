Composition and framing: where to place the subject and how to frame it

The same drawing can be dull or striking depending on where it sits in the frame. Composition is the art of guiding the reader's eye to what matters.

  • Intermediate
  • 15 min practice
  • 3 min read
  • Updated Nov 11, 2026

  • Squint at your drawing: what you see first is what the reader will see first.

The rule of thirds

  1. Step 1A grid of nine

    Mentally split your frame into three, in width and in height. You get four lines and four crossing points.

  2. Step 2The subject on a strong point

    Put the face or the important element on one of the four points rather than in the center. The image breathes and feels more alive.

  3. Step 3The horizon on a third

    Put the horizon line on the top or bottom third, never in the middle: otherwise the frame looks cut in half.

  4. Step 4Room in front of the gaze

    Leave more room on the side the character looks or moves toward. Pressed against the edge they face, they seem stuck.

Tip

The center isn't forbidden: it makes a stable, solemn or face-to-face image. Choose it on purpose, not by default.

Shot sizes

  • Wide shot: the whole setting, the characters small. To set a scene.
  • Full shot: the whole character, head to toe. To show a pose or an action.
  • Medium shot: from the head to the waist. The dialogue shot.
  • Close-up: the face. For an emotion.
  • Extreme close-up: an eye, a hand, a detail. For a moment of tension.

Guiding the eye

  1. Step 1Contrast attracts

    The eye goes first to where the contrast is strongest: a light face on a dark background, a black spot in a light setting.

  2. Step 2Lines that point

    Background lines, an outstretched arm, a gaze: everything that points leads the eye. Make them converge on your subject.

  3. Step 3Less detail around

    Keep the detail on the subject and simplify the rest. A busy background steals the attention.

Tip

Squint at your drawing: what you see first is what the reader will see first.

Studying a pro's panel

Mangaka learn by taking apart other artists' work. A pro's panel is a free composition lesson.

  1. Step 1Find the subject

    What does the panel want to show? A face, a gesture, a place? Write it down in one word.

  2. Step 2Trace the structure

    On a layer or a copy, trace the horizon, the main lines and the grid of thirds in a few strokes. See where the subject falls.

  3. Step 3Find the values

    Fill the big areas in three tones: white, gray, black. You'll see how the contrast leads the eye.

  4. Step 4Redo it differently

    Redraw the panel changing a single thing: the shot, the angle or where the subject sits. Compare what you lose and what you gain.

Tip

Do it with the panels that stay with you: they have the most to teach you.

The coach's tips

  • Make three small thumbnails before each illustration, with three different compositions, and keep the best one.
  • Flip your drawing horizontally: an unbalanced composition jumps out.
  • When a character is cut by the edge of the frame, cut between two joints, mid-thigh or mid-arm, never right at the knee or the wrist.
  • Avoid tangents: two contours that just barely touch are confusing. Overlap them clearly or move them apart.
  • Before tracing a character in the app, decide on its final framing: full shot, medium shot or close-up.

Practice right now

Once the lesson is read, draw this exercise: it takes about 15 minutes.

Practice exercise

Draw the same character in three frames of the same size: a wide shot, a medium shot and a close-up, placing their face on a third's point each time.
