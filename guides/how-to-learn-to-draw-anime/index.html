How to learn to draw anime: a roadmap from zero

To learn to draw anime, practice the foundations before the style: control your lines, draw simple 3D shapes, build a mannequin, then heads and faces, then bodies in motion. Draw 10 to 20 minutes every day, alternate exercises with drawings you enjoy, and study references instead of only copying them.

Key takeaways

Can anyone learn to draw anime?

Yes. Drawing is a skill, not a gift: the people who draw well are the ones who practiced the right things for long enough. Your first drawings will look stiff and lopsided, like everyone's, and that is part of the process, not a sign that you lack talent.

What makes the difference is the order in which you learn. Many beginners start by copying their favorite characters line by line and get stuck after a few months, because they never learned what is under the lines. Anime is a style applied to a construction: learn the construction, and the style becomes much easier.

What to learn first

Start with your hand, not with characters. Being able to draw a straight line, a clean circle and an ellipse in one stroke makes everything after it easier. Then learn to see in 3D: boxes, cylinders and spheres that turn in space.

Those shapes are the building blocks of everything else. A head is a sphere with a jaw, a rib cage is an egg, a pelvis is a box and a limb is a cylinder. Once you can draw them from any angle, characters stop being flat.

A 9-week roadmap from shapes to dynamic poses

Here is an order that works, in three phases of three weeks. It is the order of the SketchAI program, and you can follow it with a pencil and a sketchbook. Spend about a week on each step, and do not wait to be perfect before moving on: you will come back to each skill again and again.

  1. Week 1, lines, shapes and precision: control your hand and draw simple shapes.
  2. Week 2, the box method and perspective: imagine a volume turning in space. See how to draw a box in perspective.
  3. Week 3, from shapes to the mannequin: turn a character into an assembly of simple volumes, as in the anime body tutorial.
  4. Week 4, the head as a volume: turn a head in space with the anime face method.
  5. Week 5, facial features: place the eyes, the nose, the mouth and the ears on the volume of the face.
  6. Week 6, difficult angles: stop depending on the front view alone, with the head from any angle.
  7. Week 7, torso and pelvis: understand the central structure of the body.
  8. Week 8, arms, legs, hands and feet: build the limbs without drawing them as flat lines.
  9. Week 9, gesture and dynamic poses: go from a stiff mannequin to a living character, with the anime poses tutorial.

A 20-minute daily routine

Short and daily wins. A 20-minute session is long enough to make progress and short enough to never skip, and your hand keeps what you practiced from one day to the next. Use a timer so you do not overthink each part.

  1. Warm-up, 1 minute: lines, circles and ellipses to loosen your hand.
  2. Volumes, 4 minutes: boxes, cylinders and volumes in perspective.
  3. Technical exercise, 11 minutes: the topic of your week, like heads, eyes or hands.
  4. Reference study, 4 minutes: draw from a manga panel or a photo, looking for the construction.
Tip

On busy days, do only the warm-up and the volumes. Ten minutes still count, and keeping the habit matters more than any single session.

Copying, tracing and references: what actually helps

Copying and tracing drawings you love is not forbidden; it teaches you how artists simplify a face and how confident lines feel. It only becomes a trap when it is the only thing you do. After tracing or copying a drawing, rebuild it with its construction and draw it again without looking: that is where the learning happens. Our guide is tracing bad? explains how to do it well.

References are just as important. Professionals use photos, mirrors and other drawings all the time, to understand a pose or a fold, not to copy them. Study a reference for its line of action, its proportions and its volumes, then draw from that understanding.

How to stay motivated

Progress in drawing is slow from one day to the next and huge from one month to the next. Keep every drawing, date your sketchbook pages and compare them every few weeks: seeing the difference is the best motivation there is.

Mix the exercises with fun. After a week of boxes, draw a character you love as a reward, using what you practiced. Keep a streak, draw with friends or online challenges, and remember that a bad drawing still trained your hand.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to learn to draw anime?

It depends on how often you practice. With 10 to 20 minutes a day, most beginners see clear progress within a few weeks, and drawing characters comfortably from imagination takes months of regular practice.

Can I teach myself to draw anime?

Yes. Follow a structured order, from shapes and volumes to heads, faces and bodies, practice a little every day and compare your drawings with references to find what to fix.

Should I learn realism before drawing anime?

You do not need realistic rendering, but you do need its foundations: volumes, proportions and perspective. They are what make anime characters look solid instead of flat.

Do I need a tablet to learn to draw anime?

No. A pencil, an eraser and paper are enough to learn everything in this roadmap. Many artists keep drawing on paper even after they start working digitally.

What should I draw first as a beginner?

Lines, circles and ellipses for a few days, then boxes and cylinders, then a simple mannequin. Your first faces will come in a few weeks, on top of those shapes.
